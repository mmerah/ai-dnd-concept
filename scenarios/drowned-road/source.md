# The Drowned Road

The Drowned Road is the causeway that carries pilgrims from the mainland village of Tarn's End out to the shrine town of Saint Ferrant, three miles across open tidal flats. It is walkable for about four hours either side of low water; outside that window the flats fill and the road disappears under two fathoms of grey tide. Pilgrims who misjudge the crossing are found in the reeds a week later, bloated and unrecognizable, and every family at Tarn's End can name at least one.

The causeway surface is packed cockle-shell and rammed clay, laid centuries ago by hands nobody living remembers, and it holds firm even where the flats around it are soft enough to swallow a cart to the axle. Wooden distance-posts mark every quarter mile, numbered from the Tarn's End side, and pilgrims are told never to walk past post nine without checking the sky for the first grey line of returning water on the horizon.

At the Tarn's End end of the causeway stands the Bell House, a squat stone tower with a single bronze bell hung open to the weather. Its keeper is Ovid Sarn, a heavy, silent man of about fifty who rings the tide bell twice a day, once at the turn to ebb and once at the turn to flood, and who has not slept indoors since the winter his younger brother Wren drowned trying to beat the water home.

Halfway across the flats, on the only rock that stands clear of the tide at every hour, sits the Lantern Chapel, a small chapel of black, non-local stone that pilgrims use as a waypoint and a shelter if the tide catches them short. Its lamp burns in the high window every night, summer storm or winter fog, and is lit by nobody the villagers of Tarn's End will name aloud.

Three miles out, where the flats rise back into dry ground, the crossing ends at Saint Ferrant, a shrine town of perhaps four hundred souls built around the landing stair and the old sea-facing sanctuary. Saint Ferrant himself is said to have drowned reaching this shore on foot, refusing a boat, and the town that grew up around his landing has lived off pilgrim traffic ever since.

## Tarn's End and Ovid Sarn

Tarn's End is a fishing village of forty-odd houses, a smokehouse, and one inn, the Ebb Tide, where the innkeeper Coral Hask keeps a chalked tide table behind the bar because half her custom is pilgrims waiting out the water. Everyone in Tarn's End can recite the tide by feel, and everyone avoids Ovid Sarn, not from dislike but because his grief has calcified into something the village finds easier to leave alone.

Ovid Sarn rings the bell from a platform at the Bell House's top, timing the strokes by a brass hourglass his brother Wren carved notches into as a boy. He has not missed a ringing in eleven years, but his eyesight is failing and he has begun losing count of the notches in fog, which is why three pilgrims have drowned crossing at the wrong hour this past season alone, more than in any year Coral Hask can remember.

Ovid wants someone to walk the causeway with him for a season, or in his place, because he no longer trusts his own count and will not say so to anyone in Tarn's End. He keeps his brother Wren's oilskin coat hung on a peg in the Bell House and will show it to anyone who asks after Wren, along with the story of the crossing that killed him, which Ovid has never told the same way twice.

Behind a loose brick in the Bell House's north wall, unknown even to Ovid, sits a small bronze key shaped like a flatfish, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with salt. It was Wren Sarn's, though nobody now living remembers him carrying it, and it fits nothing in the Bell House itself.

## The Lantern Chapel

The Lantern Chapel is built of a black stone that takes no barnacle and grows no lichen, unlike every other surface on the flats, and pilgrims who shelter inside during a mistimed crossing describe the air as dry and strangely warm even in a gale. Its single room holds a stone altar, a ring of unlit votive niches, and a narrow stair at the rear that villagers insist has always been sealed with rubble, though the rubble is loose enough to clear by hand in an afternoon.

The chapel's high window lamp is fed by an oil that does not smell like any fish or whale oil sold in Tarn's End, sweeter and faintly resinous, and its flame never gutters even in the wind that comes off the flats at the turn of the tide. Old pilgrims say the lamp has burned every night since Saint Ferrant's own drowning, and that a night without it burning is a night the causeway takes someone.

Behind the chapel's altar, a false panel of the same black stone swings inward on a pivot worn smooth by use, not age, revealing the true stair down into the dark below. The panel shows no seam from the front and no pilgrim guidebook mentions it; only someone who already knows to press the altar's left corner, or who finds the sooty handprints worn into that corner, will ever find it.

## The Flooded Crypt

Below the Lantern Chapel, reached by the hidden stair or, once a year, by the tide itself, lies a crypt cut into the rock the chapel stands on. For all but a handful of days it sits half-drowned, its lower steps submerged in black standing water that never fully drains, and the air smells of salt, cold stone, and something sweetish underneath that nobody has identified.

Once each year, on the lowest spring tide of the season, the water drains from the crypt entirely for perhaps six hours, and the reliquary of Saint Ferrant stands revealed on its dry stone plinth at the crypt's heart. This year's uncovering falls within the coming fortnight, and Saint Ferrant's town has already begun its preparations for the Rite of the Uncovering, when the reliquary is carried up to the sanctuary and shown to the pilgrims gathered for it.

The crypt's outer door, reached from the flats at the chapel's seaward foot, is an iron grate sealed with a fish-shaped lock that has not been opened by that route in a generation; every living memory of the crypt's access runs through the hidden stair instead. The grate's lock is a twin to the bronze key hidden behind the loose brick in the Bell House, though nobody now living has connected the two.

The reliquary of Saint Ferrant is a chain of drowned silver holding a single clouded glass bead, said to be the last breath the saint drew before the tide took him, sealed by whoever built this crypt so that it would never be opened by force, only by the tide's own hand once a year. Saint Ferrant's town holds that the causeway's tide has run kindly only in years the reliquary was properly shown; the last year it was not raised, four pilgrims drowned in a single season.

## The Reed Shallows

North of the causeway, where the tidal flats give way to a stand of saltmarsh reed taller than a person, lies the stretch Tarn's End calls the Reed Shallows, or more darkly the Widow's Reeds, because it is where the drowned wash up and snag a week after the tide takes them. Fishing boats avoid netting there out of superstition, though the reeds themselves hide dry hummocks that never flood, solid enough to camp on.

A cold firepit, three days old, sits on one of the driest hummocks in the Widow's Reeds, ringed with foreign coin filings and a coil of waxed rope of a kind no chandler in Tarn's End sells. Whoever camped there left in a hurry, leaving behind a torn oilcloth map of the flats with the Lantern Chapel circled twice and a tide-table scrawled in a hand nobody in Tarn's End recognizes.

## The Cockle Path

Local shellfish-gatherers know a second route across part of the flats, the Cockle Path, a low ridge of packed shell and sand that stays just above the standing water for an hour or two after the causeway itself has flooded, though it only runs from the Reed Shallows partway toward the Lantern Chapel before the deep channel swallows it. It is marked, to those who know to look, by driftwood stakes driven at knee height and weathered nearly to the color of the mud around them.

Fenn Orr is the last of Tarn's End's cockle-gatherers still working the flats regularly, a wiry woman in her sixties who has walked the Cockle Path since childhood and knows exactly how long it holds past the causeway's own cutoff. She wants payment in coin or salt for guiding anyone across it, and she wants whoever has been fouling her cockle beds near the Reed Shallows, digging where no gatherer digs, found and stopped.

## Saint Ferrant and Prue Ilsend

Saint Ferrant, the town, keeps its sanctuary on the bluff above the landing stair, tended by Warden Prue Ilsend, a spare, exacting woman who has organized the Rite of the Uncovering for eleven years running and intends this year's to go without incident. She wants the reliquary carried up from the crypt on the coming spring tide exactly as tradition holds, and she wants to know why strangers have been asking after the crypt's other entrance in her own harbor taverns this past week.

Prue Ilsend keeps the town's own tide records, older and more exact than anything in Tarn's End, and will trade a look at them, including the notation of every year the reliquary was not raised and what followed, for real help securing the crypt before the rite. She does not yet know the hidden stair exists; she has always assumed the tide itself was the only way in.

## Mara Voss, the Lamplighter

Mara Voss is the reason the chapel lamp never goes dark: she is not a myth Tarn's End invents to avoid an answer but a living woman, once a novice at Saint Ferrant's sanctuary, who chose forty years ago to keep the Lantern Chapel's watch alone rather than take vows in the town. She lives in a dry alcove off the hidden stair, unseen by causeway traffic, and refills the lamp from oil she renders herself out of flats-kelp, which is why it smells like nothing sold on land.

Nobody at Tarn's End has laid eyes on Mara Voss in a decade, and the villagers' refusal to name the lamp's keeper is less superstition than an old, uneasy agreement to leave her alone; only the sootmarks on the altar's hidden panel and a fresh set of small, dry-shod footprints on the crypt stair are there to prove she is real to someone who has never met her. She wants the crypt and the reliquary kept exactly as they have always been kept, and she has noticed, in the last month, that someone has been trying the outer grate from the seaward side at night.

## Cassian Dell

Cassian Dell is a relic broker out of the mainland ports, hired on behalf of a private collector to bring back the reliquary of Saint Ferrant before this year's Rite of the Uncovering puts it back under a generation of superstition and guard. He has been camped in the Widow's Reeds for three days, working the fish-shaped lock on the crypt's outer grate by night and retreating before the tide or the causeway traffic can catch him, and the firepit and oilcloth map in the reeds are his.

Cassian Dell does not yet know the hidden stair from the chapel exists, only the sealed outer grate, and his lockpicks have made no progress against a mechanism built to answer one specific key. If he learns the stair is there, or is given cause to think brute force through the chapel above will work faster than the lock below, he will stop working quietly and start working fast, and Mara Voss alone will not be enough to stop him reaching the reliquary before the tide does.

## Consequences

If the tide bell is rung wrong, early, late, or not at all, Tarn's End loses another pilgrim to the flats within days, and Ovid Sarn's standing in the village, already thin, breaks entirely; he will not forgive himself and will not be reasoned with afterward. Finding and returning Wren Sarn's bronze key to Ovid, and telling him plainly what it opens, is the one thing that has ever visibly steadied him.

If Cassian Dell's camp in the Widow's Reeds is found before he is confronted, he abandons the outer grate and moves to force the chapel's altar directly, on the theory that the villagers' silence about a lamplighter means there is an easier way in than the lock he cannot beat. Warning Mara Voss, or Prue Ilsend, before that happens is the only way either of them meets him on their own terms rather than his.

If the reliquary is carried out of the crypt by anyone other than Prue Ilsend's own rite, or opened by force rather than the tide, Saint Ferrant's town takes it as the sign that broke the causeway's old truce with the water, and the season's tides begin running early and violent enough that the four-hour crossing window starts shrinking by the week. Returning it to the rite, intact and on time, is the only thing anyone in this story believes will stop that.

If the hidden stair behind the chapel altar is found and used before the annual uncovering, the crypt can be reached and the reliquary reached months early, dry-shod, without waiting on the tide at all, for good or ill depending on whose hands find the panel first.
